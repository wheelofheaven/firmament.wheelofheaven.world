03 / In the details

Small marks.
Whole meanings.

A diacritic should feel like part of the letter, and the letter like part of the sentence.

Firmament extends Jost’s own shapes. Dots, breves, and macrons share the weight and proportions of the letters around them. Half rings are drawn as open circular strokes; schwa follows the curved construction of Jost’s e.

07 / In small print

A face for staying a while.

Display character, with room for a long thought.

24 px / Regular

The space between words

Reading is an act of attention. A familiar shape becomes a sound, a sound becomes a word, and a word opens a world. Typography lives in that brief passage, giving each thought enough room to arrive.

When an unfamiliar letter appears, the sentence should still feel continuous. The marks may change; the rhythm remains.

19 px / Regular + Italic

A shared vocabulary

Across languages, a line above a vowel or a dot below a consonant can carry a distinction that ordinary spelling leaves out. The terms šāmayim, ḥayyīm, and ʾĕlōhīm bring those distinctions into Latin letters.

Firmament began with these small interruptions in reading: a fallback character that sat a little too high, a mark that belonged to another typeface. Its additions aim to make the typography feel whole again.

16 px / Regular + Bold

Notes in the margin

Form and function. The family retains the geometry and proportions of Jost while extending its scholarly vocabulary. Regular and bold each have a corresponding italic.

A growing set. Version 0.1 adds 60 Unicode mappings to full upstream Jost, including uppercase partners and combining marks. It is a starting point for further design and review.

Open by design. The font source, build scripts, and coverage audit are available to study, modify, and improve under the Open Font License.

10 / Origins & intentions

A new branch.
A familiar root.

Firmament Sans is an independent scholarly extension of Jost, designed by Owen Earl at indestructible type.

The project began while reading Wheel of Heaven’s essays and etymologies. Jost fit the character of the site, but some transliterated letters required a different font. Firmament keeps the design that was already working and extends its reach.

The pilot adds scholarly Latin characters using Jost’s own accent and letter components, along with newly constructed half rings. Original Latin and Cyrillic forms remain, and each addition is built in regular, italic, bold, and bold italic.

The name points to the shared sky above a collection of languages and traditions. The work belongs to an open process: reproducible builds, a corpus-based inventory, and visual review.

What is included in the pilot?

Latin and Cyrillic coverage from full Jost, additional scholarly Latin forms, half-ring letters, schwa, and four combining marks below. Precomposed additions and their canonical decompositions are checked with HarfBuzz.

What is still being developed?

Broader IPA and modifier coverage, Egyptological letters, additional orthographies including Vietnamese, general accent stacking, and variable-font output. Optical placement and kerning of new characters remain open to review.

Does it include Hebrew or Greek?

Firmament is a Latin/Cyrillic family. Hebrew, Greek, cuneiform, and East Asian scripts need suitable companion fonts. Latin transliteration and original-script coverage are different things.

Can I use it in a commercial project?

Yes. The Open Font License permits use, embedding, modification, and redistribution subject to its terms. Include the copyright and license when redistributing the font. The font cannot be sold on its own.

How can I contribute?

Open an issue or a pull request in the font repository. Include the Unicode character, a sample word, the font style, and the size where you noticed the problem. The corpus audit provides a starting list of missing forms.
